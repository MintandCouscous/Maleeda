28 August · Friday · 8 pm · Our Home, Shadnagar
Manja
Hyderabad's answer to Haldi

Azhar's family sends the turmeric over first — mixed with sandalwood and rosewater into a paste the colour of marigolds — and that is what opens the evening. Not flowers, not sweets. This. It travels from his house to hers, and the moment it arrives, the Manja begins. Meanwhile, on the other side of the city, the same paste is being worked into Azhar's hands by his women. Two ceremonies, same night, same ritual, one city apart. Tradition's way of starting them together even before they're in the same room.

The women of Zeenath's family apply it in turns — oldest first, that part is not negotiable. Hands, face, feet. Each round comes with a quiet dua: for health, for ease, for whatever the woman saying it most wants for her. From tonight, she doesn't step out of the house again until Sunday's Nikah. The house holds her. That's the tradition, and we're keeping it.

A little history

The word "Manja" is specific to the Deccan — elsewhere you'll hear "Haldi," "Pithi," or "Ubtan." Different names, the same root: turmeric applied to the bride and groom before the wedding. Turmeric (Curcuma longa) has been part of South Asian ceremonies for at least two thousand years. Its original appeal was partly medicinal — it's naturally antibacterial and anti-inflammatory, and its use before a wedding made practical sense when people would be in close contact for several days.

In the Deccani courts — the Qutb Shahis in Golconda, the Asafia dynasty in Hyderabad — the Manja became more elaborate. The Hyderabadi version added sandalwood and rosewater to the turmeric paste: sandalwood cools, rosewater softens, turmeric does its thing. The custom of Azhar's family sending the paste to Zeenath's house first — and the two ceremonies happening simultaneously in separate homes — is specifically Deccani. It's a way of beginning together even when apart.

The post-Manja seclusion (Zeenath stays home from tonight until Sunday's Nikah) is older than the ceremony itself. In older Hyderabadi families this period lasted a week. Here it's two days. The length isn't the point. The threshold is.

29 August · Saturday · 9 am · Our Home, Shadnagar
Mehendi
& Choodi

Same house as last night — daylight now, breakfast instead of dinner, a lot less turmeric to worry about. Today's whole plot: someone paints your hands, you let them.

Several henna artists on hand, so no queue eating your whole morning — wander over, pick a pattern, get it done between cups of chai. Choodi happens alongside it, bangles chosen purely on vibes. Zeenath included.

A little history

The henna plant (Lawsonia inermis) is native to North Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. The practice of applying its crushed, dried leaves as body art is documented in ancient Egypt; its introduction to South Asia came through medieval trade routes from the Middle East.

The Qutb Shahi court at Golconda in the 16th and 17th centuries is documented as one of the great patrons of mehendi as an art form. Court records and surviving miniature paintings show professional henna artists as part of the royal household, specifically for pre-wedding ceremonies. The Golconda style that emerged — fine, geometric, more restrained than Rajasthani patterns, more architectural than Bengali — shaped what became distinctively Hyderabadi henna.

The tradition of hiding the groom's name within the design for him to find appears in historical accounts from the Deccan more specifically than from other regions. The Choodi (bangles) are added alongside the mehendi in Hyderabadi custom — traditionally green and gold, in configurations that vary by family. These bangles stay on through the Nikah.

30 August · Sunday · 12 noon · Heritage Palace, Attapur
Nikah
& the Jalwa

A Nikah is one of the shortest ceremonies you will ever attend. Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty. A sermon, a proposal, an acceptance, a contract. No rings exchanged mid-ceremony, no walking down an aisle — just words, witnesses, and a signature that changes everything.

What happens in the khutbah?+

The Qari opens with three Quranic verses, then translates. The same three verses appear in Nikah khutbahs across the Sunni world — standardised in the Abbasid period, roughly the 9th century CE. They aren't random selections: Surah An-Nisa 4:1 addresses the shared origin of husband and wife ("He created from it its mate"). Surah Al-Imran 3:102 instructs the couple to maintain awareness of God. Surah Al-Ahzab 33:70–71 asks for precise and honest speech. Together they function as a contract preamble — not just an invocation but a direct instruction for what the marriage should be.

After the Quran: a short sermon, then the proposal, acceptance, and dua. Total time: fifteen to twenty minutes.

Why are they in separate rooms?+

His side, her side, seated apart — tradition, not logistics. The contract passes through intermediaries: a proposal spoken on Azhar's behalf, carried to Zeenath by her Wali, witnessed by two men present in the room with her.

It rests entirely on one moment: Qubool Hai — "I accept." Said three times, out loud, clearly. Zeenath says it three times; Azhar will have already said his. That's it. Done.

What happens at the Jalwa?+

The Jilwa and Arsi Mushaf are specifically Deccani Muslim traditions — they're not universal to all Sunni practice and their origins are traced to the Bahmani and Qutb Shahi courts. First, the Jilwa: Zeenath's female relatives hold a cloth around her and lower it slowly, revealing her in full bridal dress to Azhar's side for the first time. Then the Arsi Mushaf: one shawl is drawn over both of them, and a mirror is held so their first look at each other as husband and wife is seen as a reflection. Indirect, witnessed, shared. The mirror acknowledges that what they're looking at is larger than a face.

Guests then do Salaami — elders first, always. Collect your Maleeda on the way out.

What do guests actually do?+

During the khutbah: listen and stay seated. When the dua is read: say Ameen out loud, together. When Qubool Hai is confirmed: clap, ululate, say Mubarak. This is the moment. Let it be loud.

A little history

A Nikah is one of the most legally precise religious ceremonies in existence. The requirements haven't changed since the 7th century: Ijab (proposal), Qubool (acceptance), two Muslim male witnesses, a Wali (guardian for the bride), and an agreed Mahr (dower). That's the complete list. The simplicity is the point.

The Mahr is often misunderstood. It is not a brideprice paid to the bride's family — in Islamic law it belongs entirely to the bride, cannot be given to anyone else, and cannot be taken back. It's her financial entitlement and safety net, established by the contract. The amount is agreed before the ceremony and here remains private.

The two-room structure — his side, her side, with the proposal passing through intermediaries — preserves the formal structure of a contract: one party makes an offer, the other party accepts, both in the presence of witnesses. The bride's Qubool Hai is spoken in the room with her Wali and witnesses. "I accept," said three times. In classical Islamic jurisprudence, a statement repeated three times has distinct legal weight. Her three Qubool Hais are her signature.

Reception
The evening they've been waiting to throw

This one isn't about ritual at all. It's just the two of them, married now, wanting everyone they love in the same room at once. Full formalwear, real dinner, no rush.

And if there's one meal in Hyderabad a wedding gets judged by, it's this one. Come hungry.

A little history

The Walima is a Sunnah — one of only a handful of customs the Prophet Muhammad specifically encouraged for a wedding. The hadith on it are direct: a feast after marriage is not just permitted but recommended. The word comes from the Arabic walm — companionship, gathering. A Walima makes the marriage publicly known, and community knowledge is itself a form of protection for the union.

Hyderabadi biryani at the Walima is not optional, and the dish has a specific history in this city. The dum pukht method — layering rice and meat in a sealed vessel, cooking slowly over indirect heat — was brought to South Asia by the Mughals in the 16th century. What became Hyderabadi biryani was developed in the kitchens of the Asafia dynasty in the 18th century, and refined under Nizam Asaf Jah I. The Hyderabadi version differs from Mughlai biryani in its use of shorter-grain rice, distinct local spicing, and a charcoal cooking method that produces a drier, more aromatic result. The Nizams' court recipes were guarded. Heritage Palace takes its version seriously.

The Salaami — the procession of blessings from guests to the couple — follows the meal. Order is fixed: elders first. This isn't hierarchy for its own sake. In Islamic tradition, the dua of someone who has lived longer carries a specific weight. Junior guests follow after.

A note on the name

Zehar

Ze·har — portmanteau of Zeenath and Azhar, coined three months after they met. Also: the Urdu word for poison. Three months was precisely how long Azhar had to google this. He did not. He is now legally, spiritually, and logistically bound by it.

October 2021 — they met. By January, someone had already decided this needed a name. The word that arrived was Zehar. Whether this was foresight, a very good joke, or a soft warning dressed as romance, the record does not show.

What followed was four years of: the same shows watched in different cities at the same time, calls that outlasted dinner, and enough Air India bookings between them to have opinions about which route has the better cabin crew. Several telecom companies quietly flourished. Neither of them noticed.

Today they are in the same city, the same room, the same family. Azhar cannot say he wasn't warned. He can, and does, say it was entirely worth it.
