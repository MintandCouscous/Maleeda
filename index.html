Manja
Hyderabad's answer to Haldi

Azhar's family sends the turmeric paste over first, mixed with sandalwood and rosewater. When it arrives at Zeenath's house, the Manja begins. The same paste is applied at both homes on the same night, by the women of each family working simultaneously. The two ceremonies run in parallel across the city so the couple begins the ritual together before they share the same room.

The paste goes on in rounds. Hands, face, feet. Music and dancing follow, and by that point everyone in the room will have yellow on them. Come dressed for it.

A little history

The word "Manja" is specific to the Deccan. Elsewhere in South Asia the same ritual is called "Haldi," "Pithi," or "Ubtan." Turmeric (Curcuma longa) has been used in South Asian pre-wedding ceremonies for at least two thousand years. Its original appeal was partly medicinal: it is naturally antibacterial and anti-inflammatory, and its use before a wedding made practical sense when people would be in close contact for several days.

In the Deccani courts (the Qutb Shahis in Golconda, the Asafia dynasty in Hyderabad) the Manja became more elaborate. The Hyderabadi paste adds sandalwood and rosewater to the turmeric: sandalwood cools, rosewater softens. The custom of Azhar's family sending the paste to Zeenath's house first, with both ceremonies happening simultaneously in separate homes, is specifically Deccani in origin.

After the Manja, Zeenath enters a period of seclusion at home until the Nikah on Sunday. In older Hyderabadi families this period lasted up to a week. Here it is two days.

Mehendi
& Choodi

Same house as the night before, now in daylight. Breakfast is on the table. Multiple henna artists are set up through the morning so there is no long wait. Sit down, pick a design, and drink chai while it is applied. The morning runs loose and there is no fixed order.

Alongside the henna, the Choodi takes place. The women of Zeenath's family choose and stack her glass bangles in the traditional green-and-gold combination. The bangles go on today and stay on through the Nikah the following morning.

A little history

Henna has been winding its way into celebrations for thousands of years, arriving in Hyderabad not as an import but as something that simply belonged here, the way chai and biryani did. It came through North Africa and the Arab world and settled so naturally into the Deccan that no one needed to explain it. It was already home.

The Qutb Shahi court at Golconda in the 16th and 17th centuries was a major patron of mehendi as an art form. Court records and surviving miniature paintings show professional henna artists as part of the royal household, specifically for pre-wedding ceremonies. The Golconda style that emerged is fine and geometric, more restrained than Rajasthani patterns and more architectural than Bengali. It shaped what became distinctively Hyderabadi henna.

The tradition of hiding the groom's name within the bride's design for him to find is documented in historical accounts from the Deccan more specifically than from other regions. The Choodi are added alongside the mehendi in Hyderabadi custom: traditionally green and gold, they stay on through the Nikah.

Nikah
& the Jalwa

A Nikah runs fifteen to twenty minutes. The ceremony consists of a khutbah (short sermon including three Quranic verses), a proposal, an acceptance, and a signed Nikahnama. The Nikahnama is the marriage contract: it names both parties, the Mahr, and the witnesses, and it is signed by Azhar, Zeenath, her Wali, and the two witnesses. After that it is a legal and religious marriage in full. No rings exchanged during the ceremony, no aisle, no processional.

What happens in the khutbah?+

The Qari recites three Quranic verses before the sermon. Each has a specific purpose within the ceremony.

"O believers, be mindful of God as He deserves, and do not die except in a state of willing surrender."
Surah Al-Imran · 3:102
Taqwa, the word usually translated as "mindfulness of God," is better understood as a continuous state of consciousness: awareness that one is seen and that choices carry weight. The verse asks that this awareness be held at its fullest pitch, not as a passing sentiment but as a permanent condition. The Nikah begins here because everything that follows assumes it.
"O humanity, be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it created its mate, and from both of them spread countless men and women."
Surah An-Nisa · 4:1
The Arabic word for spouse, zawj, means counterpart or the other of a pair. The verse describes creation as beginning with a single soul from which its mate was drawn; from those two, all of humanity followed. Marriage is not incidental to this account. It is written into the structure of how the world was made. When this verse is read at a Nikah, it is saying: this is not a new thing happening. This is a very old thing completing itself.
"O believers, be mindful of God and speak the truth. He will set your deeds right for you and forgive your wrongs. Whoever obeys God and His Messenger has won something immense."
Surah Al-Ahzab · 33:70-71
The Arabic qawlan sadida is usually translated as "speak the truth" or "speak rightly," but the root sadid carries additional meaning: straight, sound, well-aimed. Speech that hits what it intends. The verse arrives precisely before the words of the ceremony because the proposal, the acceptance, and the three Qubool Hais are speech acts in the strictest sense: they are not descriptions of a thing, they are the thing itself. To say Qubool Hai is to make it true.

After the three verses the Qari gives a short sermon, then the proposal, acceptance, and dua follow. Total: fifteen to twenty minutes. The room knows when the Qubool Hai lands.

Why are they in separate rooms?+

His side and her side are seated apart. The Nikah has the formal structure of a contract: one party makes an offer, the other accepts, both in the presence of witnesses. The proposal is made on Azhar's behalf and carried to Zeenath by her Wali (guardian). Two Muslim male witnesses are present in the room with her.

The moment that completes the contract is Zeenath's Qubool Hai: "I accept," said three times. In classical Islamic jurisprudence, a statement repeated three times carries distinct legal weight. Her three Qubool Hais are her signature.

What happens at the Jalwa?+

The Jalwa is a set of two Deccani traditions traced to the Bahmani and Qutb Shahi courts, performed in the evening after the Nikah luncheon.

Then the Arsi Mushaf: a single shawl is drawn over both Azhar and Zeenath and a mirror is held between them so their first look at each other as husband and wife is a reflection. The moment is shared, indirect, witnessed. There is a long tradition of this kind of first meeting in Deccani weddings: seen through a mirror, under a shared canopy, never quite direct. The indirection is not modesty. It is form.

The Salaami follows: family and guests offer their blessings to the couple in turn. Maleeda is distributed on the way out, a traditional Hyderabadi sweet made from roasted wheat, ghee, and jaggery, crumbled by hand and given at the close of a celebration. Taking it home is considered a blessing.

A little history

A Nikah has five legal requirements that have not changed since the 7th century: Ijab (proposal), Qubool (acceptance), two Muslim male witnesses, a Wali (guardian for the bride), and an agreed Mahr (dower). A ceremony performed in a living room with five people present is legally identical to one performed in a palace with five hundred.

The Mahr belongs entirely to the bride. It cannot be given to anyone else and cannot be taken back. It is her financial entitlement established by the contract, not a payment made to her family. The amount is agreed before the ceremony; here it remains private.

The two-room structure preserves the formal nature of a contract: one party makes an offer, the other accepts, both in the presence of witnesses. The bride's three Qubool Hais are spoken in the room with her Wali and witnesses. That is her signature.

Reception
The evening they've been waiting to throw

The reception is the celebration dinner. Azhar and Zeenath will be married by this point and this is the party. No ceremony, no set order of events. Dinner, music, and the full evening with everyone they love in one room.

In Hyderabad, a wedding reception is judged above all by its biryani. Heritage Palace works from the Nizami dum pukht tradition. Come hungry.

A little history

Hyderabadi dum pukht biryani is made by layering parboiled rice and marinated meat in a sealed vessel, then cooking slowly over charcoal. The technique was developed in the kitchens of the Asafia dynasty in the 18th century and refined under Nizam Asaf Jah I. It differs from Mughlai biryani in its use of shorter-grain rice, distinct local spicing, and a dryer finish. The Nizams' court recipes were closely guarded. Heritage Palace works from that tradition.

The Salaami follows the meal. Guests offer their blessings to the couple in turn. Maleeda (a traditional Hyderabadi sweet of roasted wheat, ghee, and jaggery, crumbled by hand) is distributed on the way out. Taking it home is considered a blessing.

The bird who carries the message

Why Hud-hud?

الهدهد  ·  The Hoopoe
Surah An-Naml  ·  The Quran

In the twenty-seventh surah, Hud-hud is the messenger bird of the Prophet Sulayman, present at his court among all creatures. One day he is absent. Sulayman notices immediately and says he will call him to account unless the reason is worthy.

When Hud-hud returns, he carries news no one else could have brought: he has found a kingdom in Saba ruled by a queen, a people who bow to the sun. Sulayman does not dismiss him. He writes a letter beginning with Bismillah and sends it back with the same bird. The letter changes everything.

This is a bird entrusted with the most important message of the age. He went where his king could not see. He came back with the truth. He was believed.

Mantiq al-Tayr  ·  Attar, 12th century

In Attar's great poem, the birds of the world must journey together to find the Simurgh, their true sovereign. They are afraid. They make excuses. They do not know the way.

The Hoopoe steps forward. He is the only one who has been to the court of Sulayman. He knows what it means to carry a message across impossible distance and have it received. He leads the birds through thirty mountains, each one a different demand: love, bewilderment, annihilation. Most fall away. The ones who arrive discover that the Simurgh and themselves were always the same thing.

Hud-hud is the guide because he has already been where they are going.

Mughal & Deccani poetry

In the miniature paintings of the Mughal and Deccani courts, Hud-hud appears in the margins of love poems, perched on garden walls and the edges of manuscripts, always mid-journey. He is the go-between, the one who carries letters between people who cannot reach each other. In Deccani poetry especially, he is the bird of longing, proof that two people separated by distance are still in conversation.
